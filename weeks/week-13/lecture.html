What-if analysis with the twin

The library is too cold when it opens. Its thermostat holds 17 °C at night and heats to 21 °C from 6:00 on weekdays and from 8:00 at weekends. In the observed week of February, the library was still below 20 °C for 11 of its opening hours, all of them in the morning. The facility manager asks two questions: Which schedule keeps the library warm at opening, and what does it cost?

A what-if analysis answers such questions with the twin instead of the building. The calibrated twin of Week 12 runs in a closed loop with a model of the thermostat. The thermostat switches the 60 kW of the heating on below its setpoint minus 0.5 K and off above its setpoint plus 0.5 K. Two numbers judge a schedule. The heating energy of the week counts the cost. The comfort deficit counts the degrees below 20 °C during the opening hours, multiplied by their duration:

\[E = \sum_k P_k\,\Delta t, \qquad D = \sum_{k \,\in\, \text{open}} \max\left(0,\ 20 - T_k\right)\Delta t\]

Before the twin answers what-if questions, it must reproduce what happened. With the current schedule, the twin needs 5730 kWh in the observed week, against the 5693 kWh of the heat meter. Its comfort deficit is 8.79 K h, against 9.85 K h of the library. The twin thus also knows that the library is cold in the morning.

An earlier start helps only a little: Starting at 4:00 still leaves 4.13 K h. A warmer night of 19 °C almost removes the deficit, but it needs 6075 kWh, 6.0 percent more. A third option is the optimal start. Each morning the controller asks the twin how long the heating needs to reach 20.5 °C with full power. It starts the heating when this time reaches the time left before opening. The formula is the warm-up time of Week 2:

\[t_{need} = R\,C \,\ln\frac{T_{ss} - T}{T_{ss} - 20.5}, \qquad T_{ss} = T_{out} + 60\,R\]

A worked example. At 3:00 the twin stands at 17.20 °C with 1.00 °C outside. With full power the library would settle at \(T_{ss} = 1.00 + 60 \times 0.44 = 27.40\) °C, and \(R\,C = 0.44 \times 30.41 = 13.38\) h. Reaching 20.5 °C takes \(t_{need} \)​\({}= 13.38 \ln(10.20/6.90) \)​\({}= 13.38 \times 0.391 \)​\({}= 5.23\) h. The library opens in 5 hours, less than the 5.23 hours needed, so the heating must start at once.

With the optimal start, the night setpoint no longer costs comfort. A deep setback of 13 °C with the optimal start keeps the library above 20 °C at every opening hour of the observed week and needs 5760 kWh. That is only 0.5 percent more than the cold schedule of today.

Uncertainty: Many possible weeks

The observed week is one week out of many. Next week may be colder, cloudier or fuller, and a schedule that works in one week may fail in another. The Monte Carlo method runs the model over many random inputs and counts the outcomes [3]. Each possible week here draws a daily level of the outdoor temperature, a cloud factor and a factor of the visitors:

\[\ell_0 = 2.5\,\varepsilon_0, \qquad \ell_d = 0.6\,\ell_{d-1} + 2\,\varepsilon_d, \qquad \hat{p} = \frac{\#\{\text{weeks with } D > 1 \ \text{K h}\}}{n}, \qquad \sigma_{\hat{p}} = \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}\,(1 - \hat{p})}{n}}\]

Here \(\varepsilon\) are standard normal random numbers, so neighbouring days resemble each other. The estimated probability \(\hat{p}\) of a comfort deficit above 1 K h is a share of the \(n\) weeks. Its standard error \(\sigma_{\hat{p}}\) shrinks with the square root of \(n\).

A worked example. With 13 °C at night and the optimal start, 19 of 300 possible weeks have a comfort deficit above 1 K h. The estimate is \(\hat{p} = 19/300 = 0.063\), and \(\sigma_{\hat{p}} \)​\({}= \sqrt{0.063 \times 0.937 / 300} \)​\({}= \sqrt{0.000197} \)​\({}= 0.014\). An interval of two standard errors around the estimate, from 0.035 to 0.091, covers the true probability with a confidence of about 95 percent.

Over the 300 weeks, the current schedule always leaves a deficit above 1 K h, with a mean of 7.59 K h. A warmer night of 19 °C brings the probability down to 0.067 but needs 5698 kWh per week on average. The deep setback with the optimal start reaches a similar probability, 0.063, with 5420 kWh, 4.9 percent less. The 90 percent quantile of its energy, 6092 kWh, gives the energy office the budget of a cold week.

Predictive maintenance and the remaining useful life

Jardine and colleagues describe how maintenance moved from repairs after a breakdown to work at fixed intervals. It then moved to decisions based on the measured condition, with diagnostics and prognostics [4]. Four strategies result. Corrective maintenance repairs after a failure. Preventive maintenance replaces parts at fixed intervals, whether they are worn or not. Condition-based maintenance acts when a measured condition crosses a limit. Predictive maintenance forecasts when the condition will cross the limit and plans the work before that.

StrategyWhen the work is doneOn the campus
CorrectiveAfter a failureA street light is replaced when it goes dark
PreventiveAt fixed intervalsThe air filters of the library are changed every six months
Condition-basedWhen a measured condition crosses a limitA pump is serviced when its vibration exceeds an alarm limit
PredictiveBefore the predicted crossing, with time to prepareNew battery modules are ordered before the battery reaches its end of life

The remaining useful life (RUL) is the time from now until a part can no longer do its job [5]. Si and colleagues stress that the RUL is random, so its estimate needs a distribution and not just one number. For a battery, the job ends by convention when the capacity falls to 80 percent of its rated value. For the battery of the campus that is 400 of 500 kWh.

The capacity of a battery in operation is known in two ways. At the end of every month, the battery management system estimates the usable capacity from the energy of a full discharge in daily operation, corrected to a reference temperature. Once a year, a capacity test checks this estimate: The battery is charged fully, rests and is discharged at its rated power down to its lower limit. The battery of the campus has 36 monthly estimates since its commissioning.

Inside a lithium-ion cell, many physical and chemical mechanisms wear it down. Birkl and colleagues group their effects into the loss of lithium inventory and the loss of active material of the two electrodes [6]. Seen from outside, the battery ages in two ways. Calendar ageing goes on with time, even when the battery rests, and cycle ageing grows with every charge and discharge. Xu and colleagues combine a calendar part and a cycle part in a model of the ageing of grid batteries [7]. A simple model of that kind describes the lost share of the capacity with the age \(t\) in years and the equivalent full cycles \(N\):

\[1 - \frac{Q}{Q_0} = a\sqrt{t} + b\,N, \qquad N(t) = N_{now} + 365.25\,n\,(t - t_{now}), \qquad a\sqrt{t_{end}} + b\,N(t_{end}) = 0.2\]

\(Q\) is the capacity and \(Q_0 = 500\) kWh the rated capacity. The square root describes a calendar ageing that slows down with time, and \(n\) is the number of cycles per day from now on. The model is linear in \(a\) and \(b\), so least squares fits it directly to the monthly capacity estimates. With \(x = \sqrt{t_{end}}\), the end of life is the positive root of a quadratic equation.

After three years, the battery has made 1078.3 cycles, and its capacity is 460.0 kWh. The fit gives \(a = 0.0236\) per square root of a year and \(b = 0.0000412\) per cycle, with a root mean square error of 1.48 kWh. A straight line in time fits the estimates worse, with 3.21 kWh. It also predicts the end of life after 6.44 years, in April 2030, more than two years before the 8.70 years of the model with both kinds of ageing. The straight line mistakes the fast early calendar ageing for a constant rate.

→ This formula with numbers, step by step: Lab Part B, calculator, steps 1 to 5.

A worked example. With one cycle per day from now on, the cycle ageing per year is \(k = b \times 365.25 = 0.01505\). The constant term is \(c_0 \)​\({}= b\,(1078.3 - 365.25 \times 3) \)​\({}- 0.2 \)​\({}= -0.20072\). The quadratic equation \(k x^2 + a x + c_0 = 0\) has the discriminant \(0.0236^2 \)​\({}+ 4 \times 0.01505 \times 0.20072 \)​\({}= 0.01264\). Its positive root is \(x \)​\({}= (-0.0236 + 0.11243)/0.0301 \)​\({}= 2.9512\). The end of life comes after \(x^2 = 8.71\) years, in July 2032, so the remaining useful life is 5.71 years.

The uncertainty of the remaining useful life

Thirty-six estimates with a noise of 1.5 kWh do not fix \(a\) and \(b\) exactly. Least squares also returns their covariance, and the two are strongly correlated: A larger calendar ageing comes with a smaller cycle ageing. A Monte Carlo simulation draws 2000 pairs of parameters from this uncertainty and computes an end of life for each pair:

\[\begin{pmatrix} a_i \\ b_i \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \hat{a} \\ \hat{b} \end{pmatrix} + L \begin{pmatrix} z_{1,i} \\ z_{2,i} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad L L^T = \Sigma, \qquad P_{\text{in time}}(M) = \frac{\#\{i : t_{end,i} > t_{now} + (M + 6)/12\}}{2000}\]

\(\Sigma\) is the covariance of the fit, \(L\) its Cholesky factor, and \(z\) are standard normal numbers. New modules take six months to arrive, so an order placed \(M\) months from now arrives in time if the end of life comes later than that. The latest safe order is the largest \(M\) with a probability of at least 0.95.

At one cycle per day, 90 percent of the futures reach the end of life between January 2032 and March 2033, with the median in July 2032. Modules ordered within 56 months, by July 2031, arrive in time with a probability of 0.952. The campus also considers frequency regulation, which would add half a cycle per day. Xu and colleagues assessed the ageing that such a service causes [7]. For the battery of the campus, the median end of life moves forward to December 2030, and the order must be placed within 37 months, by December 2029.

A worked example. At 1.5 cycles per day, an order placed 37 months from now, in December 2029, arrives in June 2030. Of the 2000 futures, 1952 reach the end of life after that. The probability that the modules arrive in time is \(1952/2000 = 0.976\). One month later the probability drops below 0.95.

In the field: The yearly capacity test

The monthly estimates of this lecture come from daily operation, and once a year a capacity test checks them. The International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) defines the parameters of a storage system, such as its energy capacity, and the methods that test them in IEC 62933-2-1 [8]. On site, the test takes the battery out of service for most of a day. The energy management system (EMS) must not dispatch it meanwhile, and the racks must lie within the temperatures that the test procedure of the maker asks for.

The battery is charged fully and rests for an hour, so that the voltages of its cells settle. It is then discharged at its rated power of 250 kW down to its lower limit, while a meter counts the energy. The relative deviation from the estimate of the battery management system (BMS) tells whether its estimates can be trusted:

\[\delta = \frac{E_{test}}{E_{BMS}} - 1\]

For example, a test that delivers 452.0 kWh against an estimate of 460.0 kWh gives \(\delta = 452.0/460.0 - 1 = -0.0174\), or -1.74 percent.

Learning machines for prognostics

The model of this week knows the shape of the ageing: A square root for the calendar and a straight line for the cycles. Learning machines can find such relations in data without that knowledge. Severson and colleagues cycled 124 commercial lithium-ion cells with cycle lives from 150 to 2300 cycles [9]. From the discharge voltage curves of the early cycles, before the capacity had faded, their best models predicted the cycle life with a test error of 9.1 percent.

A learned prognosis carries the conditions of its training data. The cells of that study were charged fast in a laboratory, while the battery of the campus cycles slowly with the sun. A learned model needs data from batteries like the one it judges, and its uncertainty must be stated as clearly as that of a physical model.

A prognosis that learns from many batteries

Suppose that the maker of the battery collects the capacity estimates of a thousand containers in the field. A network trained on them could predict the end of life of a new container from its first year, long before the model of this week becomes certain. The twin of the campus would then compare the two predictions and trust the learned one only where the container resembles the ones it learned from.
