Dependability: Faults, errors and failures

Avizienis and colleagues separate three terms [1]. A fault is the cause, such as a broken wire or a wrong line of code. An activated fault produces an error, a wrong internal state. A failure occurs when the error reaches the service, which then deviates from what it should deliver.

Faults have two broad origins. Development faults are made while a system is designed or programmed, and every copy of the system carries them. Physical faults arise in operation, when parts wear, break or are disturbed. The two call for different means.

MeansWhat it doesIn this week
Fault preventionAvoids faults in the design and in the partsA simple transfer logic with one clear safety rule
Fault removalFinds and removes faults before useModel checking of the transfer logic
Fault toleranceDelivers the service despite faultsTwo island controllers and a reserve in the battery
Fault forecastingEstimates how often faults lead to failuresReliability models and a fault tree

Dependability also has several attributes [1]. Reliability is the continuity of correct service, and availability is the readiness for it. Safety is the absence of catastrophic consequences for people and the environment. For the campus, the supply of the critical loads is a question of availability. A battery that energises the lines of the grid during a repair is a question of safety.

Verification: Checking every state

When the grid fails, the island controller must open the breaker at the point of common coupling (PCC). Only then may the inverter of the battery form the island grid. If the inverter formed the grid while the breaker was still closed, the battery would energise the lines of the grid. Line workers who repair the outage could be hurt. The safety rule of the transfer is therefore:

\[\square\, \neg\left(\text{inverter forms the grid} \wedge \text{breaker closed}\right)\]

The box says that the rule holds in every reachable state. Testing runs a few chosen scenarios and checks the rule in them. Model checking explores every state that a model of the system can reach and checks the rule in each one [3, 4]. If the rule fails, the model checker returns a counterexample, a path from the start to an unsafe state.

The model of the transfer logic has six variables. The first is the mode of the controller: GRID, OPENING, ISLAND, SYNC or FAULT. The others are its timer, the breaker, the inverter, the grid and a breaker that is stuck closed. One step lasts 100 ms. In each step, the grid may fail or return and the breaker may stick, and the controller reacts. Breadth-first search visits the states layer by layer, one step further each time [5].

A worked example. The first version of the logic switches the inverter to grid forming 200 ms after it has commanded the breaker to open, assuming that the breaker has opened by then. The model checker reaches 22 states, and 4 of them break the rule. The shortest counterexample takes three steps. The grid fails while the breaker is stuck closed, the controller waits two steps in OPENING, and the inverter forms the grid with the breaker closed.

The corrected version waits until the breaker reports that it is open. If the breaker is still closed after 300 ms, the controller switches the inverter off and enters FAULT. The model checker reaches 18 states, and none of them breaks the rule. This is a proof for the model, not for the real controller: The model must match the code, and its assumptions about the environment must hold.

Reliability and redundancy

Physical faults are random. A part with a constant failure rate \(\lambda\) survives a time \(t\) with the reliability \(R(t)\). Its mean time to failure (MTTF) is the inverse of the rate [6]:

\[R(t) = e^{-\lambda t}, \qquad \text{MTTF} = \frac{1}{\lambda}, \qquad R_{series} = \prod_i R_i, \qquad R_{parallel} = 1 - \prod_i \left(1 - R_i\right)\]

Parts in series must all work, so their reliabilities multiply and their failure rates add up. Parts in parallel fail only together, so their unreliabilities multiply. A k-out-of-n system works when at least k of its n parts work. Two-out-of-three voting (2oo3) is common in safety systems, because two healthy parts can outvote a wrong one. For one out of two (1oo2) and for 2oo3 of equal parts:

\[R_{2oo3} = 3R^2 - 2R^3, \qquad \text{MTTF}_{1oo2} = \frac{3}{2\lambda}, \qquad \text{MTTF}_{2oo3} = \frac{5}{6\lambda}\]

A worked example. The island supply needs the battery management system, the inverter, an island controller and the breaker, with failure rates of 0.05, 0.2, 0.1 and 0.02 per year. In series the rates add up to \(\lambda_s = 0.37\) per year, so \(R(1) = e^{-0.37} = 0.691\) and the MTTF is \(1/0.37 = 2.70\) years. One controller survives a year with \(e^{-0.1} = 0.905\). Two in parallel reach \(1 - 0.095^2 = 0.991\), and two out of three reach \(3 \times 0.905^2 \)​\({}- 2 \times 0.905^3 \)​\({}= 0.975\).

Redundancy does not help forever. Two out of three beats one part only as long as \(R > 0.5\), which for one controller means the first \(\ln 2 / 0.1 = 6.93\) years. Its MTTF is even shorter than that of one part, 8.33 years against 10. Redundant parts must therefore be tested and repaired, not just installed.

A repaired part is described by its availability, the share of time in which it works. The inverter fails on average once in five years, an MTTF of 43,800 h, and is repaired within 15 days, a mean time to repair (MTTR) of 360 h:

\[A = \frac{\text{MTTF}}{\text{MTTF} + \text{MTTR}} = \frac{43800}{43800 + 360} = 0.9918, \qquad q = 1 - A = 0.0082\]

Hidden faults and common causes

Some faults show themselves at once: An inverter that fails stops and raises an alarm. Others stay hidden until the part is needed. A broken output of an island controller is noticed only when the grid fails, or at the next proof test. With a rate \(\lambda\) of such hidden faults and a proof test every \(\tau\) years, the controller is unavailable on average for \(\lambda\tau/2\) of the time [6, 7]:

\[q_1 = \frac{\lambda\,\tau}{2}, \qquad q_{1oo2} = \beta\,\frac{\lambda\,\tau}{2} + \frac{\left((1 - \beta)\,\lambda\,\tau\right)^2}{3}\]

Two controllers are not independent. A common cause, such as a faulty firmware update or the heat of a failed fan, can stop both at once. The beta factor model gives a share \(\beta\) of all failures to such common causes [6]. The second term covers independent faults of both controllers. Its factor 1/3 instead of 1/4 reflects that both are more likely to have failed late in the test interval.

A worked example. The controllers of the campus have hidden faults at a rate of \(\lambda = 0.1\) per year, a proof test once a year and \(\beta = 0.1\). One controller is unavailable with \(q_1 = 0.1 \times 1/2 = 0.05\). The pair reaches \(q_{1oo2} \)​\({}= 0.1 \times 0.05 \)​\({}+ (0.9 \times 0.1)^2/3 \)​\({}= 0.005 \)​\({}+ 0.0027 \)​\({}= 0.0077\). The common cause dominates, so the second controller helps less than the square \(0.05^2 = 0.0025\) suggests.

Fault trees

A fault tree starts from an undesired top event and breaks it down with logic gates into basic events [8]. An OR gate passes a failure if any of its inputs fails, an AND gate only if all of them fail. The top event here is that the critical loads lose their supply in an outage of the grid. They are the server room of the library and the freezers of the laboratories, 80 kW together.

In an outage, the island fails if the battery is below its reserve or the battery management system (BMS) blocks the discharge. It also fails if the inverter is under repair, if both island controllers have failed or if the breaker fails to open. The minimal cut sets, the smallest combinations of basic events that cause the top event, are these single events, the common cause and the pair of independent controller faults. With independent basic events, the probability of a loss, the losses per year and the Fussell-Vesely (FV) importance of an event are:

\[P_{loss} = 1 - \prod_i \left(1 - q_i\right), \qquad f = f_{grid}\,P_{loss}, \qquad \text{FV}_i = \frac{q_i \prod_{j \ne i} \left(1 - q_j\right)}{P_{loss}}\]

→ This formula with numbers, step by step: Lab Part B, calculator, steps 1 to 5.

Two outages a year need the island, \(f_{grid} = 2\). The frequency \(f\) of losses is the outage rate times the probability that the island fails. The importance \(\text{FV}_i\) gives the share of the losses to which the basic event \(i\) contributes, and it shows where improvements pay off [8].

The schedules of Week 9 discharge the battery to its floor of 50 kWh in the evening. From 20:00 until it charges again before midnight, 4 hours of the day, it holds less than the reserve of 130 kWh. The reserve would carry the critical loads for one hour. An outage in these hours finds an empty battery, so \(q_R = 4/24 = 0.1667\). The BMS blocked the discharge for 35 hours of the last year, \(q_B = 0.0040\). The maker of the breaker gives \(q_K = 0.0030\) for a failure to open.

A worked example. For the current design, the five probabilities are 0.1667, 0.0040, 0.0082, 0.0077 and 0.0030. The island survives an outage with \(0.8333 \times 0.9960 \times 0.9918\) \(\times\, 0.9923 \times 0.9970 = 0.8144\). So \(P_{loss} = 0.1856\) and \(f = 2 \times 0.1856 = 0.371\) per year. The critical loads lose their supply about once in 2.7 years, and the battery below the reserve has an importance of 0.88.

A reserve rule keeps the 130 kWh in the battery at all times, so \(q_R = 0\). Then \(P_{loss} = 0.0227\) and \(f = 0.045\) per year, once in 22 years, below the target of 0.05 per year that the campus has set. In the schedules of Week 9, planning with the reserve costs 9.68 euro on the clear day and 6.97 euro on the cloudy day, about 3000 euro a year. A second controller costs 600 euro a year and each proof test 250 euro.

Runtime monitoring and the simplex architecture

The reserve rule must hold even when the schedule that drives the battery is wrong, outdated or attacked. A runtime monitor checks every command before it reaches the inverter and corrects the commands that would break the rule. Sha calls this approach using simplicity to control complexity [9]. A complex controller optimises the operation, and a simple, verified safety controller takes over whenever the complex one would leave the safe states.

The monitor of the battery allows a discharge only as far as the reserve permits:

\[d_t \le \left(e_{t-1} - e_{res}\right)\eta, \qquad e_{res} = 50 + 80 = 130\ \text{kWh}\]

Here \(d_t\) is the discharge power in hour \(t\), \(e_{t-1}\) the stored energy before it and \(\eta = 0.95\) the efficiency of Week 9.

A worked example. In the optimal clear day of Week 9, the battery holds 245.9 kWh at 19:00, and the schedule asks for 141.0 kW. The monitor allows \((245.9 - 130) \times 0.95 = 110.1\) kW and keeps 130 kWh. At 20:00 it blocks the planned 45.2 kW completely. The two interventions keep the reserve, but the day costs 1410.34 euro instead of 1379.91, partly because the import peak rises to 489.9 kW. The battery also ends the day with 80 kWh more, worth 12.00 euro at the value of stored energy of Week 9.

Planning with the reserve from the start is cheaper. The linear program of Week 9 with a lower limit of 130 kWh costs 1389.59 euro, 9.68 euro more than the optimum, and the monitor never needs to act. The simplex architecture keeps both: The optimiser plans with the rule, and the monitor stays as a safety net for the day when the plan is wrong.

The engineering side: The safety case

A safety case is a structured argument, supported by evidence, that a system is acceptably safe for a given use in a given environment [10]. The Goal Structuring Notation (GSN) draws the argument as goals, strategies, evidence and context, so that a reviewer can follow each claim to its evidence. Standards of the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) for functional safety, such as IEC 61508, give each safety function a safety integrity level (SIL) [7]. The level sets how reliable the function must be and how rigorously it must be developed and verified.

Learning machines and safety

Learning controllers, such as a policy trained by reinforcement learning (RL), can find better schedules than rules written by hand. They are also hard to verify, because their behaviour is learned rather than written. Seshia and colleagues describe the challenges of verifying such systems, from modelling their environment to specifying what the learned part should do [12].

Alshiekh and colleagues place a shield between a learning agent and its environment [13]. The shield is synthesised from a formal specification and corrects an action only if it would break the specification. The monitor of the battery is a shield of this kind: Any schedule, learned or optimised, passes through it before it reaches the inverter.

A learning battery behind a shield

Suppose that the campus trains a policy that learns from prices and forecasts when to charge and discharge the battery. Behind the shield, the policy may explore and earn money, while the reserve of 130 kWh holds in every hour. The safety case then needs evidence for the shield only, not for the learned policy.

Review cards

Select a card to turn it over.

Dependability
The ability to deliver a service that can justifiably be trusted [1].
Fault, error, failure
The cause, the wrong internal state it produces and the deviation of the service that follows [1].
Development fault
A fault made while a system is designed or programmed; every copy carries it.
Physical fault
A fault that arises in operation, when parts wear, break or are disturbed.
Model checking
The exploration of every reachable state of a model to check a property [3, 4].
Safety invariant
A condition that must hold in every reachable state, such as no grid forming with the breaker closed.
Counterexample
A path from the start to a state that breaks the property, returned by the model checker.
Reliability
\(R(t) = e^{-\lambda t}\): The probability that a part works without failure up to the time \(t\).
Mean time to failure (MTTF)
The expected time to the first failure, \(1/\lambda\) for a constant rate.
Availability
\(\text{MTTF}/(\text{MTTF} + \text{MTTR})\): The share of time in which a repaired part works.
k out of n
A system that works when at least k of its n parts work, such as two-out-of-three voting.
Hidden fault and proof test
A fault that stays undetected until the part is needed or tested; with tests every \(\tau\) the part is unavailable for \(\lambda\tau/2\) [6].
Common cause failure
A single cause that stops several redundant parts at once; the beta factor gives it a share of all failures.
Fault tree
A tree of logic gates from a top event down to basic events [8].
Minimal cut set
A smallest combination of basic events that causes the top event.
Fussell-Vesely (FV) importance
The share of the probability of the top event to which a basic event contributes.
Simplex architecture
A complex controller with a simple, verified safety controller that takes over when needed [9].
Safety case
A structured argument, supported by evidence, that a system is acceptably safe [10].
Safety integrity level (SIL)
The level of IEC 61508 that sets how reliable a safety function must be [7].
Shield
A component that corrects the actions of a learning agent only when they would break a specification [13].
