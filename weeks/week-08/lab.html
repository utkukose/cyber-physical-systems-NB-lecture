Networks and communication lab

Three parts of this interactive lab are virtual laboratories in three dimensions (3D) with the hardware in the foreground. Part A puts a Modbus bus on the bench, with a controller, three devices and an oscilloscope. Part B lets you configure the subscribers of an MQTT broker that runs on a Raspberry Pi. Part C runs the frequency loop of the microgrid through a network whose delay you set. Parts A and C also compute the formulas of the lecture step by step, and Part D asks for the values of stage 8 by hand. In the calculators every intermediate value is rounded, and the next step continues with the rounded value. The results of Parts A, B and C can be copied into the Colab notebook. Each laboratory first shows its room. Start moves the view to the bench, and the view returns to the room at the end. The laboratories work best with a mouse or a touchpad on a computer.

Part A: The Modbus bench

The bench holds the controller of the battery container on the left and three devices on the mounting rail. They are the container meter with address 1, the battery inverter with address 2 and the battery management system (BMS) with address 3. A twisted pair, the RS-485 bus, runs from the controller from device to device, and the probe of the oscilloscope sits on the bus. The controller reads 10, 20 and 40 registers from the three devices, and each device answers after 5 ms.

Play four rounds. In each round, first predict the time of the poll in milliseconds with the formula of the lecture. Then press Poll and watch the frames on the cable and on the oscilloscope. The poll runs in slow motion, and the controller measures its real duration. One round hides a fault on the bus: Find it and click the part of the bench that repairs it. In the last round, choose the baud rate yourself.

Part B: Topics at the broker

The bench of the control room holds the MQTT broker of the campus, a Raspberry Pi joined to an Ethernet switch. On the mounting rail behind it, nine devices publish twelve topics, such as campus/b2/meter/power. Three clients subscribe: The data logger on the bench, the dashboard on the wall and the battery controller at the end of the rail.

Play six missions. In the first five, type the topic filter of the named subscriber, or several filters separated by semicolons, and press the button. The devices publish once, and the broker forwards each message to the subscriber only if a filter matches. The mission is solved when the subscriber receives exactly the topics it needs. The sixth mission asks for the packets of a quality of service.

Part C: Frequency control over the network

The microgrid runs as an island. Its battery container stands next to the car park. The microgrid controller in the rack measures the frequency at the point of common coupling and sends power set points to the battery inverter. Between them sits a network emulator, a box that delays every message by the time set on its dial. When three cars plug in at the chargers, 66 kW of load switch on, and the battery has to answer.

Find the largest delay at which the frequency still settles. Set the delay and the gain below the view and press Plug in the cars. The trace on the screen shows the frequency deviation. The run ends as settled, as growing or as tripped, when the under-frequency relay disconnects the loads below 47.5 Hz. Try as many delays as you need, then enter your estimate of the boundary.

Part D: By hand: Stage 8 of the microgrid

The battery container polls a meter with 10 registers, an inverter with 20 and a battery management system with 40 at 19200 baud, each with a reply delay of 5 ms. The Colab notebook gives a poll cycle of 129.6 ms; check it to one decimal. Then add the loop delay through the campus server: Half of a sampling period of 0.2 s, 0.15 s up, 0.05 s in the controller and 0.10 s down. Finally, compute the share of the delay margin of 1.072 s that the loop keeps, in percent to one decimal.
