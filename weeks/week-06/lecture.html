Estimation: Combining noisy measurements

Two sensors of the same quantity never agree exactly. When their errors are independent and their standard deviations \(\sigma_A\) and \(\sigma_B\) are known, the best linear combination weighs each reading with the inverse of its variance [1]:

\[\hat{x} = \frac{x_A/\sigma_A^2 + x_B/\sigma_B^2}{1/\sigma_A^2 + 1/\sigma_B^2} = x_A + K\,(x_B - x_A), \qquad K = \frac{\sigma_A^2}{\sigma_A^2 + \sigma_B^2}, \qquad \hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{1}{1/\sigma_A^2 + 1/\sigma_B^2}\]

The combined standard deviation is always smaller than the smaller of the two. The second form already shows the idea of the Kalman filter: Start from one value and move a fraction \(K\) of the way toward the other.

→ These formulas with numbers, step by step: Lab Part B, calculator, step 1.

A worked example. The calibrated sensor of the library of Week 4 reads 21.4 °C with \(\sigma_A = 0.3\) K. The sensor of the thermostat reads 20.8 °C with \(\sigma_B = 0.5\) K. The gain is \(K = 0.09/(0.09 + 0.25) = 0.2647\), so \(\hat{x} \)​\({}= 21.4 \)​\({}+ 0.2647 \times (20.8 - 21.4) \)​\({}= 21.24\) °C. The combined variance is \(1/(11.11 + 4) = 0.0662\), a standard deviation of 0.257 K.

Check your understanding. A sensor with \(\sigma = 1\) K is combined with a perfect sensor whose \(\sigma\) is 0. What is the combined estimate?

The Kalman filter: Predict and correct

A state that changes over time needs a weighted mean at every step. The filter of Kalman alternates two steps [1]. It predicts the state with a model and lets the uncertainty grow. It then corrects the prediction with a measurement, weighted by the two uncertainties.

The battery management system of the campus learns its state of charge from two sources [4]. Coulomb counting integrates the measured power \(u_k\), but its current sensor reads 5 kW too little, so the count drifts. The voltage gives the state of charge through the open-circuit voltage of Week 2, with a noise of 0.01. The filter combines both at every minute:

\[\hat{x}^-_k = \hat{x}_{k-1} - c\,u_k, \qquad P^-_k = P_{k-1} + Q, \qquad c = \frac{\Delta t}{\eta E}\]
\[K_k = \frac{P^-_k}{P^-_k + R}, \qquad \hat{x}_k = \hat{x}^-_k + K_k\,(z_k - \hat{x}^-_k), \qquad P_k = (1 - K_k)\,P^-_k\]

\(P\) is the variance of the estimate. \(Q\) is the variance that the state gains per step through the errors of the model, and \(R\) is the variance of a measurement, here \(0.01^2 = 10^{-4}\). A large \(Q\) trusts the measurements, a small one the model. For a constant \(Q\) and \(R\), the gain settles at a steady value [5]:

\[K_\infty = \frac{P^-_\infty}{P^-_\infty + R}, \qquad P^-_\infty = \frac{Q + \sqrt{Q^2 + 4\,Q\,R}}{2}\]

→ These formulas with numbers, step by step: Lab Part B, calculator, steps 2 to 5.

A worked example. At 17:00 the battery management system believes 0.95 with \(P_0 = 0.05^2 = 0.0025\), while the true state is 0.90. In the first minute it measures 50.17 kW, so \(\hat{x}^- = 0.95 \)​\({}- 50.17/(60 \times 475) \)​\({}= 0.9482\), and \(P^- = 0.002501\) with \(Q = 10^{-6}\). The voltage gives \(z = 0.9008\). The gain is \(K \)​\({}= 0.002501/(0.002501 + 0.0001) \)​\({}= 0.9616\), and the estimate becomes \(0.9482 \)​\({}+ 0.9616 \times (0.9008 - 0.9482) \)​\({}= 0.9026\), close to the true 0.8981.

The gain then falls minute by minute and settles at 0.0951. Over the four hours, coulomb counting ends with an error of +0.091. The voltage alone has a root-mean-square (RMS) error of 0.0107, and the filter with \(Q = 10^{-6}\) reaches 0.0037.

The Kalman filter with matrices

The offset of the current sensor is constant, and a filter with one state cannot learn it. The Kalman filter extends to a vector of states with matrices [1]. The state becomes \(\mathbf{x} = (SoC, b)\), the state of charge and the offset \(b\) of the sensor in kW:

\[\hat{\mathbf{x}}^- = A\,\hat{\mathbf{x}} + B\,u, \qquad P^- = A P A^T + Q, \qquad K = P^- H^T \bigl(H P^- H^T + R\bigr)^{-1}\]
\[\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \hat{\mathbf{x}}^- + K\,(z - H\hat{\mathbf{x}}^-), \qquad P = (I - K H)\,P^-, \qquad A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & c \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad H = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\]

The offset never appears in a measurement directly. It shows itself as a drift between coulomb counting and the voltage: In one hour, an offset of 5 kW shifts the count by \(5/475 = 0.0105\). The filter attributes this persistent disagreement to \(b\).

A worked example. With a variance of 100 kW² for the offset at the start, the filter estimates it at -5.80 kW by 18:00, -4.71 kW by 19:00 and -5.70 kW by 21:00. Its standard deviation falls to 0.33 kW. The RMS error of the state of charge drops to 0.0030, below the best filter with one state.

Check your understanding. Why can the filter estimate the offset although no sensor measures it?

State estimation of the campus

The control room of the campus receives 13 power readings for six feeders at 19:00. Each feeder has a meter at the main switchboard with \(\sigma = 1.5\) kW and a meter in its building, read over the network, with \(\sigma = 2.5\) kW. The main meter adds the sum of all six feeders with \(\sigma = 3\) kW.

State estimation finds the six powers that fit all readings best, each weighted by its uncertainty. Schweppe and Wildes introduced this estimate by weighted least squares (WLS) for power systems [2]:

\[\hat{\mathbf{x}} = (H^T W H)^{-1} H^T W\,\mathbf{z}, \qquad W = R^{-1} = \mathrm{diag}(1/\sigma_i^2), \qquad \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{z} - H\hat{\mathbf{x}}\]

Here \(H\) has 13 rows: One for each feeder meter, one for each building meter and a row of ones for the main meter. With only two meters of one quantity, the estimate reduces to the weighted mean of the first section.

A worked example. The meters of B3 read 103.3 kW at the switchboard and 107.9 kW in the building. With the weights \(1/1.5^2 = 0.4444\) and \(1/2.5^2 = 0.16\), their weighted mean alone is \((45.91 + 17.26)/0.6044 = 104.52\) kW. The main meter reads 585.5 kW for the sum of all six feeders, and with it the estimate of B3 becomes 103.93 kW.

Check your understanding. How many readings does the state estimate of the campus have beyond its unknowns?

Bad data: Residuals and the chi-square test

A wrong reading, such as that of an open current transformer, pulls the estimate away and leaves large residuals. If all errors are normal, the weighted sum of the squared residuals follows a chi-square distribution with \(m - n\) degrees of freedom, for \(m\) readings and \(n\) unknowns [6]:

\[J = \mathbf{r}^T W\,\mathbf{r} = \sum_i \frac{r_i^2}{\sigma_i^2} \sim \chi^2_{m-n}, \qquad r_i^N = \frac{|r_i|}{\sqrt{\Omega_{ii}}}, \qquad \Omega = R - H\,(H^T W H)^{-1} H^T\]

If \(J\) exceeds the 99 percent quantile of this distribution, the readings contain bad data. The meter with the largest normalized residual \(r_i^N\) is the most likely culprit. It is removed, and the estimate is repeated.

A worked example. With 13 readings and 6 unknowns, the threshold is \(\chi^2_{7;\,0.99} = 18.48\). The clean readings give \(J = 8.04\). When the current transformer of the B3 feeder meter opens, the meter reads 0 kW and \(J\) jumps to 1626.32. The largest normalized residual, 40.2, points at that meter. Without it, \(J = 7.50\), and B3 is estimated at 105.22 kW.

Small errors hide in the noise. An offset of the B3 feeder meter is detected only from 11 kW on, with \(J = 19.55\), while an offset of 10 kW gives \(J = 17.05\) and passes.

Check your understanding. Why is the meter with the largest normalized residual removed, and not the one with the largest residual?

The engineering side: Trusting an estimator

An estimator is only as good as its model and its noise values. Engineers check it during operation with its own innovations, the differences \(z - H\hat{\mathbf{x}}^-\) between each reading and its prediction. For a consistent filter, the innovations look like white noise with the variance \(H P^- H^T + R\) that the filter expects.

CheckWhat it reveals
The mean of the innovations lies far from zeroAn offset that the model does not contain
The innovations are larger than expectedA value of Q or R that is too small, or a fault
The innovations are correlated in timeA missing state or a wrong model
The variance P keeps growingA state that the measurements cannot observe

Numerical care matters too: The covariance must stay symmetric and positive, which a symmetric form of the update keeps in floating point. In state estimation, every meter needs another meter that checks it. A reading that nothing else checks can be wrong without leaving any residual.

In everyday life: Dead reckoning at sea

Before satellite navigation, a navigator advanced the position of a ship with its speed and heading, which slowly drifted. Whenever a lighthouse came into view, the navigator corrected the position toward the sighting, the more the clearer the view. This is the predict and correct of a Kalman filter.

Learning to estimate

A Kalman filter needs a model and the variances \(Q\) and \(R\). When the model is only partly known, the gain can be learned from data instead. KalmanNet keeps the structure of the Kalman filter and computes its gain with a recurrent neural network that is trained on recorded trajectories [7]. Its authors report that it copes with non-linear dynamics and with errors of the model better than classic filters.

For the battery of the campus, a learned estimator could capture the ageing of the cells and the effect of the temperature on the voltage. The linear model of Week 2 ignores both. It still needs the checks of this week: Its innovations must look like noise, and its estimates must pass the same tests.

A gain that learns the cells

Suppose that the team records the voltage, the current and the temperature of the battery for a year, with a laboratory measurement of the true state of charge every month. A small recurrent network learns to correct the gain of the filter for cold mornings and for aged cells. The classic filter keeps running beside it as a reference.

Review cards

Select a card to turn it over.

Inverse-variance weighting
Combines readings with the weights \(1/\sigma_i^2\); the result is better than each reading alone [1].
Kalman filter
Predicts a state with a model and corrects it with each measurement, weighted by the two uncertainties [1].
Prediction
\(\hat{x}^- = A\hat{x} + Bu\) with the variance \(P^- = APA^T + Q\).
Kalman gain
\(K = P^-H^T(HP^-H^T + R)^{-1}\), the share of the measurement in the correction.
Process noise Q
The variance that the state gains per step through the errors of the model.
Measurement noise R
The variance of a measurement.
Steady gain
For constant \(Q\) and \(R\), the gain settles at \(K_\infty\) [5].
Coulomb counting
Integrates the measured current or power; an offset of the sensor makes it drift [4].
Augmented state
A constant such as the offset of a sensor added to the state, so that the filter learns it.
Weighted least squares
\(\hat{x} = (H^TWH)^{-1}H^TWz\), the state estimate of a power system [2].
Residual
\(r = z - H\hat{x}\), what the estimate cannot explain.
Chi-square test
\(J = r^TWr\) compared with the quantile of \(\chi^2_{m-n}\) detects bad data [6].
Normalized residual
\(|r_i|/\sqrt{\Omega_{ii}}\); the largest one identifies the most likely bad reading.
Innovation
\(z - H\hat{x}^-\), the surprise of a filter, which should look like white noise.
