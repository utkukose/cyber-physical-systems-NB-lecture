Feedback control lab

Two parts of this interactive lab are virtual laboratories in three dimensions (3D) with the hardware in the foreground. Part A tests the frequency controller of the battery on a hardware-in-the-loop bench. Part C opens the inverter cabinet of the battery. Part B computes the formulas of the lecture step by step, and Part D asks for the values of stage 5 by hand. In the calculator every intermediate value is rounded, and the next step continues with the rounded value. The results of Parts A and C can be copied into the Colab notebook. Each laboratory first shows its room. Start moves the view to the bench or the cabinet, and the view returns to the room at the end. The laboratories work best with a mouse or a touchpad on a computer.

Part A: The hardware-in-the-loop bench

The hardware-in-the-loop bench tests the frequency controller of the battery before it meets the island. A real-time simulator computes the island of the campus every 2 ms. Its model holds the inertia of 80 kW·s/Hz, the damping of 12 kW/Hz of the loads and the inverter that follows within 0.2 s. It sends the frequency to the real controller as an analog voltage, and the controller sends its power command back. An oscilloscope shows the frequency and the power of the battery for 30 s after a load step.

Set the three gains below the view, choose the load step and press Run. Your missions: First, run proportional control alone at two values of Kp and compare the final deviation with -ΔP/(D + Kp). Second, find an integral gain that returns within 20 mHz in less than 7 s without an overshoot above 10 mHz. Third, raise Ki until the oscillation no longer dies away. Keep Kp and Kd fixed, make one run on each side of the limit and compare with the Routh-Hurwitz limit of the lecture. The decay compares the second dip with the first: Below 1 the oscillation dies away.

Part B: The controller, step by step

The calculator follows the formulas of the lecture for the chosen gains, after a step of 66 kW. It computes the remaining error of P control and the natural angular frequency and damping ratio of PI control without the lag. It then computes the stability limit of the integral gain with the lag of 0.2 s and the noise of a raw derivative. The figure draws the deviation for the chosen gains, with the lag.

Part C: The inverter cabinet of the battery

The inverter cabinet of the battery has two sections. The open power section holds three power modules with insulated-gate bipolar transistors (IGBT), their heat sinks and fans, the capacitors of the direct-current link and the filter chokes. The closed control section carries a touch panel with the command of the controller, the power of the battery and the limit of 250 kW. The controller runs with Kp = 125 kW/Hz and Ki = 60 kW/(Hz·s). Its proportional and integral terms use the true frequency of the model. The derivative term, when it is switched on, uses the readings of the frequency meter of Week 4, 100 a second with a noise of 25 mHz.

Choose the event, the anti-windup and the derivative below the view and press Run. Your missions: First, trip the wind turbine with the anti-windup off and on, without the derivative, and compare the overshoot and the settling time with the lecture. Second, start the three cars with a derivative of Kd = 40 kW·s/Hz, once with raw and once with filtered readings. Compare the noise of the derivative term with the formula. The noise is measured over the last 10 s of a run. It differs a little from the formula, because each run draws other random readings and the noisy command also moves the frequency itself.

Part D: By hand: Stage 5 of the microgrid

After the step of 66 kW, a P controller with $K_p = 200$ kW/Hz leaves a deviation: Compute it in Hz to three decimals. A PI controller has $K_p = 125$ kW/Hz and $K_i = 240$ kW/(Hz·s): Compute its damping ratio without the lag to two decimals. Finally, a derivative term of $K_d = 20$ kW·s/Hz is added to $K_p = 125$: Compute the limit of the integral gain with the lag of 0.2 s.
