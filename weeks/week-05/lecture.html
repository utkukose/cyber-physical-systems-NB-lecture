Feedback: Proportional control and its error

A feedback controller compares a measured value \(y\) with its set point \(r\) and acts on the error \(e = r - y\) [1]. The frequency controller of the battery measures the deviation \(\Delta f\) from 50 Hz, so its error is \(e = -\Delta f\), and its command \(u\) is the power of the battery. The simplest law is proportional (P) control, \(u = K_p\,e\), which Week 2 met as the droop of the battery.

The island of Week 2 and the inverter of the battery form the plant of the loop. The inverter follows its command with the time constant \(\tau_a = 0.2\) s:

\[M\,\dot{\Delta f} = P_b - \Delta P_{load} - D\,\Delta f, \qquad \tau_a\,\dot{P}_b = u - P_b\]

The ramp limit of 50 kW a second of Week 4 applies to the set points of the energy management, and the frequency response bypasses it. With \(u = -K_p\,\Delta f\), the deviation settles where the battery and the loads together cover the step [3]:

\[\Delta f_{ss} = -\frac{\Delta P_{load}}{D + K_p}, \qquad T_{cl} \approx \frac{M}{D + K_p}\]

→ These formulas with numbers, step by step: Lab Part B, calculator, step 1.

A worked example. Three cars start charging with 66 kW. The droop of Week 2 with \(K_p = 125\) kW/Hz settles at \(-66/137 = -0.482\) Hz within about \(80/137 = 0.584\) s. Doubling the gain to 250 kW/Hz leaves \(-66/262 = -0.252\) Hz. A proportional controller always leaves an error, because it needs a deviation to produce any power.

Integral action: Back to 50 Hz

An integral term adds up the error over time. As long as any deviation remains, it keeps changing the power, so the deviation can only settle at zero. Together with the proportional term, it forms a proportional-integral (PI) controller:

\[u(t) = K_p\,e(t) + K_i \int_0^t e(t')\,dt'\]

Without the lag of the inverter, the loop then follows a differential equation of second order. Its natural angular frequency \(\omega_n\) and its damping ratio \(\zeta\) describe how fast and how calmly the deviation returns [1]:

\[M\,\ddot{\Delta f} + (D + K_p)\,\dot{\Delta f} + K_i\,\Delta f = 0, \qquad \omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{K_i}{M}}, \qquad \zeta = \frac{D + K_p}{2\sqrt{M\,K_i}}\]

A damping ratio near 1 returns without overshoot. Smaller values return faster but swing past 50 Hz.

→ These formulas with numbers, step by step: Lab Part B, calculator, steps 2 and 3.

A worked example. With \(K_p = 125\) kW/Hz and \(K_i = 60\) kW/(Hz·s), \(\omega_n = \sqrt{60/80} = 0.866\) rad/s. The damping ratio is \(\zeta \)​\({}= 137/(2\sqrt{4800}) \)​\({}= 137/138.56 \)​\({}= 0.989\). The simulation with the lag of the inverter shows a nadir of -0.413 Hz and a return within 20 mHz of 50 Hz after 6.05 s. With \(K_i = 240\), \(\zeta = 137/277.13 = 0.494\): The deviation returns faster but overshoots by 0.122 Hz.

Stability: How far the integral may go

A loop is stable when every deviation dies away. A linear loop is stable when all roots of its characteristic equation, its poles, have negative real parts [1]. With the lag of the inverter and all three terms, the characteristic equation of the island is of third order:

\[M\,\tau_a\,s^3 + (M + D\,\tau_a + K_d)\,s^2 + (D + K_p)\,s + K_i = 0\]

For a cubic \(a_3 s^3 + a_2 s^2 + a_1 s + a_0\) with positive coefficients, the Routh-Hurwitz criterion requires \(a_2 a_1 > a_3 a_0\). The integral gain therefore has an upper limit. At the limit, the loop oscillates with the angular frequency \(\omega^*\):

\[K_i < K_i^* = \frac{(M + D\,\tau_a + K_d)(D + K_p)}{M\,\tau_a}, \qquad \omega^* = \sqrt{\frac{D + K_p}{M\,\tau_a}}\]

→ This formula with numbers, step by step: Lab Part B, calculator, step 4.

A worked example. For \(K_p = 125\) kW/Hz and \(K_d = 0\), the coefficients are \(a_3 = 80 \times 0.2 = 16\), \(a_2 = 80 + 12 \times 0.2 = 82.4\) and \(a_1 = 137\). The limit is \(K_i^* = 82.4 \times 137/16 = 705.55\) kW/(Hz·s). At the limit, the loop oscillates with \(\omega^* = \sqrt{137/16} = 2.926\) rad/s, a period of \(2\pi/2.926 = 2.147\) s. The design value of 60 stays a factor of 11.8 below the limit.

The integral removes the error but costs stability. Every further lag in the loop lowers the limit, and Week 8 adds the delay of a network to the same loop.

Derivative action: Damping and noise

A derivative term reacts to the rate of change of the error. It acts before the deviation has grown, and with the other two terms it forms a proportional-integral-derivative (PID) controller. In the frequency loop it behaves like extra inertia, which is why a battery with this term is said to provide synthetic inertia. Inverters that take part in the control of the frequency in such ways are called grid-supporting converters [4]:

\[u = K_p\,e + K_i \int_0^t e\,dt' + K_d\,\dot{e}, \qquad M_{eff} = M + K_d\]

In the characteristic equation, \(K_d\) adds to the coefficient of \(s^2\), so it also raises the stability limit. With \(K_d = 40\) kW·s/Hz, the limit grows from 705.55 to \(122.4 \times 137/16 = 1048.05\) kW/(Hz·s).

The derivative has a price: It amplifies noise. The frequency meter of Week 4 delivers 100 readings a second with a noise \(\sigma\) of 25 mHz. The difference of two consecutive readings, divided by \(T_s = 0.01\) s, has a noise of \(\sqrt{2}\,\sigma/T_s\) [5]. A moving average of \(N\) readings and a difference over \(\Delta t\) reduce it:

\[\sigma_{\dot f} = \frac{\sqrt{2}\,\sigma}{T_s}, \qquad \sigma_{\dot f,\,filtered} = \frac{\sqrt{2}\,\sigma/\sqrt{N}}{\Delta t}\]

→ This formula with numbers, step by step: Lab Part B, calculator, step 5.

A worked example. Raw readings give \(1.414 \times 0.025/0.01 = 3.536\) Hz/s of noise, so \(K_d = 40\) puts \(40 \times 3.536 = 141.4\) kW of noise on the command of the battery. The moving average of 7 readings and a difference over \(\Delta t = 0.1\) s give \(1.414 \times 0.00945/0.1 = 0.1336\) Hz/s, or 5.35 kW. In the model with an ideal derivative, \(K_d = 40\) raises the nadir after 66 kW from -0.413 to -0.355 Hz.

Check your understanding. Why must the derivative term work on filtered readings?

Saturation and windup

Every actuator saturates. The inverter of the battery delivers at most 250 kW. When the controller asks for more, the integral keeps growing although the power no longer changes. This integrator windup stores a large value that must be unwound later, and the frequency overshoots [1].

A simple anti-windup stops the integration while the command lies beyond its limit and the error would drive it further:

\[\dot{I} = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } |u| > u_{max} \text{ and } e\,u > 0 \\ K_i\,e & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}, \qquad u_{cmd} = \min\bigl(u_{max},\ \max(-u_{max},\ u)\bigr)\]

A worked example. In island mode, the wind turbine trips at 240 kW. With \(K_p = 125\) and \(K_i = 60\), the frequency falls by 1.502 Hz to 48.50 Hz, and the command reaches 358.9 kW. Without anti-windup, the stored integral drives the frequency 0.372 Hz above 50 Hz, and it settles within 20 mHz only after 17.20 s. With anti-windup there is no overshoot, and it settles after 10.16 s.

Check your understanding. What does an anti-windup do?

The engineering side: Tuning and testing a controller

Engineers rarely derive the gains from a model alone. Ziegler and Nichols proposed rules from a single experiment. The gain of a P controller is raised until the loop oscillates steadily, and the ultimate gain \(K_u\) and the period \(T_u\) of this oscillation give the settings [6].

Controller\(K_p\)\(T_i = K_p/K_i\)\(T_d = K_d/K_p\)
P\(0.5\,K_u\)--
PI\(0.45\,K_u\)\(T_u/1.2\)-
PID\(0.6\,K_u\)\(T_u/2\)\(T_u/8\)

Driving a real loop to the edge of stability is risky. Åström and Hägglund replaced the experiment by a relay that makes the loop oscillate with a small, controlled amplitude. Their method is the basis of the autotuners of many industrial controllers [7]. Before a controller meets the grid, it is tested against a real-time simulation of its plant on a hardware-in-the-loop bench.

In everyday life: The shower

A shower with a long pipe answers the tap only after a few seconds. Whoever turns the tap again before the water has changed swings from too cold to too hot and back. This is an integral action that is too strong for the lag of its loop.

In the field: The controller as settings of the inverter

On site, the controller of stage 5 is not programmed as formulas but entered as settings of the battery inverter. Inverters give the proportional term as a droop \(s\): A change of the frequency by \(s\,f_n\) changes the power by the rated power \(P_n\) [3]. The integral appears as an integral time. The derivative appears as an inertia constant \(H\), because it acts like the inertia of a rotating machine:

\[K_p = \frac{P_n}{s\,f_n}, \qquad T_i = \frac{K_p}{K_i}, \qquad H = \frac{K_d\,f_n}{2\,P_n}\]

The inverter of the campus has \(P_n = 250\) kW at \(f_n = 50\) Hz. Its factory droop of 5.0 percent is the gain \(250/(0.05 \times 50) = 100\) kW/Hz. With an integral gain of 50 kW/(Hz·s), the integral time is \(T_i = 100/50 = 2.0\) s. A derivative of 25 kW·s/Hz gives \(H = 25 \times 50/(2 \times 250) = 2.5\) s. Inverters take these values to one decimal, and a load step test checks them before the controller goes into service.

Learning to control

Reinforcement learning finds a control policy by trial and error: It applies actions, observes a reward and improves the policy. Recht compares such methods on the linear quadratic regulator, the simplest benchmark of optimal control. Methods that use a model of the plant need far fewer trials than methods that learn without one [8].

On the campus, trials on the real island are not acceptable. Learning therefore happens on a model, on the hardware-in-the-loop bench or on the digital twin of Weeks 12 and 13. A learned controller still has to respect the limits of this week: The stability limit, the power of the inverter and the noise of its sensors.

A tuner that learns the seasons

Suppose that the team lets a small model propose the gains of the battery controller for each season, from a year of recorded load steps. A supervisor accepts a proposal only when the Routh-Hurwitz condition holds with a margin of at least 5. The bench then tests the proposal before it reaches the island.
