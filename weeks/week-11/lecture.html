Threats and attack surfaces

Information security pursues three goals: Confidentiality, integrity and availability. An office network puts confidentiality first. A cyber-physical system turns the order around. A wrong command or a frozen control loop can damage equipment or hurt people, while a disclosed reading usually cannot. The guide of the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) on operational technology (OT) therefore weighs integrity and availability highest [3].

Cárdenas and colleagues divide the attacks on a control system into two classes [1]. Deception attacks send false readings or false commands. Denial-of-service (DoS) attacks block or delay the true ones. Teixeira and colleagues describe what an attacker needs as three resources [4]. Model knowledge tells how the system works, disclosure lets the attacker read its data, and disruption lets the attacker change or block data. Each attack of this week uses a different combination.

The best known attack on a cyber-physical system is Stuxnet. It reached the controllers of a uranium enrichment plant and changed the speed of the centrifuges. Meanwhile it showed the operators recorded values of normal operation [5, 6]. Surveys collect the threats, attacks and defences of many kinds of cyber-physical systems [7]. The campus offers smaller targets, but the same kinds of entry points.

False data injection

The control room of Week 6 estimates the six feeders of the campus from thirteen meters by weighted least squares (WLS). The weights are \(W = R^{-1}\), the inverses of the variances of the meters. The test value \(J\) sums the weighted squared residuals and raises a bad-data alarm above the 99 percent quantile of the chi-square distribution:

\[\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \left(H^T W H\right)^{-1} H^T W \mathbf{z}, \qquad \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{z} - H\hat{\mathbf{x}}, \qquad J = \mathbf{r}^T W \mathbf{r}\]

Liu, Ning and Reiter showed that this test has a blind direction [8]. An attacker who knows \(H\) adds the false data \(\mathbf{a} = H\mathbf{c}\) to the readings. The readings then look exactly like those of a system whose state is \(\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{c}\). The estimate moves by \(\mathbf{c}\), and the residual does not change at all:

\[\mathbf{z}_a = \mathbf{z} + H\mathbf{c} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \hat{\mathbf{x}}_a = \hat{\mathbf{x}} + \mathbf{c}, \qquad \mathbf{r}_a = \mathbf{r}, \qquad J_a = J\]

Such a false data injection (FDI) is called hidden or stealthy. A change of one meter alone, in contrast, breaks the consistency of the readings and raises \(J\).

→ These formulas with numbers, step by step: Lab Part B, calculator, steps 1 to 4.

A worked example. Two feeders have one meter each, \(z_1 = 120\) and \(z_2 = 80\) kW, and the main meter reads their sum \(z_3 = 206\) kW. All three have \(\sigma = 2\) kW. The mismatch is \(u = z_1 + z_2 - z_3 = -6\) kW. Least squares gives \(\hat{x}_1 \)​\({}= (2z_1 - z_2 + z_3)/3 \)​\({}= 122.00\) kW and \(\hat{x}_2 = 82.00\) kW. The residuals are \(u/3\), \(u/3\) and \(-u/3\), so \(J = u^2/(3\sigma^2) = 36/12 = 3.00\). That lies below 6.63, the threshold with one degree of freedom.

An attacker adds 20 kW to \(z_1\) alone. The mismatch becomes 14 kW, \(J = 196/12 = 16.33\), and the test raises an alarm. A second attacker adds 20 kW to \(z_1\) and to \(z_3\), the first column of \(H\). The mismatch stays −6 kW and \(J\) stays 3.00, while \(\hat{x}_1\) rises by the full 20 kW to 142.00 kW.

The control room of the campus sheds the dormitory B3 when its estimate exceeds 150 kW, its rated load. At 19:00 the estimate is 103.93 kW and \(J = 8.04\) against the threshold 18.48. An attacker who changes only the B3 feeder meter must add 68.65 kW, because the estimate follows that meter with a share of 0.671. The test value then jumps to 658.61, a certain alarm.

The hidden attack adds 50 kW to the B3 feeder meter, the B3 building meter and the main meter, the third column of \(H\). The test value stays at 8.04, the estimate of B3 rises to 153.93 kW, and the control room sheds B3 without any alarm. Any hidden shift above 46.07 kW would do the same.

Attacks on control loops: Replay and denial of service

A control loop gives the attacker two more options. A denial-of-service attack blocks the messages of the loop. The controller notices that no measurement arrives, for example with a watchdog as in Week 7, and falls back to a safe mode. A delay is subtler: Beyond the delay margin of 1.072 s of Week 8, the frequency loop of the island becomes unstable.

A replay attack records measurements of normal operation and plays them back while the attacker acts on the plant, as Stuxnet did. Mo and Sinopoli showed why this works [9]. In steady operation the replayed readings are real readings of the same system, so they have the same statistics as live ones. Every test that looks only at the readings, the chi-square test included, sees nothing wrong.

The island of the campus shows the danger. The battery holds the frequency with its droop of Weeks 2 and 5. It reads the frequency meter every \(\Delta t = 0.1\) s and sets its power from the measurement \(y_t\). Over one step, the frequency deviation follows the swing equation of Week 2:

\[f_{t+1} = \alpha f_t + \beta \left(P_t - d_t\right), \qquad \alpha = e^{-D\Delta t / M} = 0.9851, \qquad \beta = \frac{1 - \alpha}{D} = 1.2407 \ \text{mHz per kW}, \qquad P_t = -K y_t\]

Here \(d_t\) is the change of the load, \(M = 80\) kW·s/Hz, \(D = 12\) kW/Hz and \(K = 125\) kW/Hz. An attacker inserts a small box into the line of the frequency meter. It records the frequency from 20 to 60 s and replays the recording from 60 s. At 80 s a forged command starts three cars, the load step of 66 kW of Week 2.

The battery sees a calm frequency and does not respond. The true frequency falls with the time constant \(M/D = 6.67\) s and reaches the protection limit of 47.5 Hz at 84.0 s. The island trips 4.0 s after the step, as the island without droop did in Week 2.

Mo and Sinopoli proposed a physical watermark [9, 6]. The battery adds a small secret random power \(\omega_t\) to its setpoint, \(P_t = -K y_t + \omega_t\). One step later the measured frequency must have moved by \(\beta\,\omega_t\). A recording cannot know the current watermark, so a replay does not show this response. The detector measures the response as the slope of the movement \(m_{t+1}\) against the watermark over the last \(N\) steps:

\[m_{t+1} = y_{t+1} - (\alpha - \beta K)\,y_t, \qquad g = \frac{\sum \omega_t\, m_{t+1}}{\beta \sum \omega_t^2}, \qquad \text{alarm when } g < 0.5\]

In normal operation \(g\) stays close to 1, and under a replay it falls to about 0. After an alarm the battery switches to its own measurement of the frequency.

A worked example. Four steps have the watermark \(\omega = 8, -6, 10\) and \(-4\) kW. Their movements are \(m = 9.1, -8.2, 12.0\) and \(-3.5\) mHz, and \(\beta = 1.24\) mHz per kW. The sum of the products is \(72.8 + 49.2 + 120.0 + 14.0 = 256.0\), and \(\beta \sum \omega^2 \)​\({}= 1.24 \times 216 \)​\({}= 267.84\). So \(g = 256.0/267.84 = 0.96\): The frequency answers the watermark. Replayed movements of \(m = -2.1, 4.0, 1.3\) and \(-5.2\) mHz give \(g = -7.0/267.84 = -0.03\): An alarm.

A watermark of 10 kW with a window of 5 s finds the replay at 62.6 s, 2.6 s after it starts. The battery then holds the frequency at about −0.50 Hz after the step, close to the −0.482 Hz of the droop of Week 2. The price is a noisier frequency: Its jitter in normal operation grows from 5.53 to 21.45 mHz. A smaller watermark needs a longer window. With 2 kW, a window of 20 s raises a false alarm at 22.0 s. A window of 30 s finds the replay at 72.7 s, with a jitter of 6.81 mHz.

Defences in depth

No single defence stops every attack, so a cyber-physical system builds layers. The first layer keeps attackers out. The International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) groups the devices of a control system into zones in its series IEC 62443. Traffic between zones passes only through defined conduits. Part 3-3 of the series lists the security requirements that each zone must meet [10]. The second layer makes forged data visible, the third detects what still passes, and the fourth lets the plant survive an attack.

Message authentication answers the forged command. The controller and the inverter share a secret key \(K\). Each frame carries a counter and a tag. The tag is the first 8 bytes of the hash-based message authentication code (HMAC) of the frame and the counter, with SHA-256 as the hash function [11]:

\[\text{tag} = \text{HMAC}_K\left(\text{frame} \,\|\, \text{counter}\right)_{1..8}, \qquad \text{accept when the tag is right and the counter} > \text{the last counter}\]

A forged value makes the tag wrong, and a replayed frame carries an old counter. A random tag of 64 bits is right with a chance of \(2^{-64}\), about \(5.4 \times 10^{-20}\). The frame of the setpoint grows from 8 to 20 bytes. On the bus of Week 8 at 19200 baud, a poll cycle of the meter, the inverter and the battery management system then takes 170.83 ms instead of 129.58 ms.

Protected meters answer the hidden injection. Kim and Poor showed that a small set of meters that the attacker cannot change suffices [12]. Such meters carry a tag or measure with their own protected link. A hidden attack must then leave every protected meter alone:

\[H_P \mathbf{c} = \mathbf{0} \ \text{has no solution } \mathbf{c} \neq \mathbf{0} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \operatorname{rank} H_P = n\]

Here \(H_P\) holds the rows of the protected meters and \(n\) is the number of states. The campus estimates six feeders, so at least six meters must carry a tag. Of the 1716 sets of six meters, 256 leave no hidden attack, for example the six feeder meters. A tag on the main meter alone does not help: The attacker can raise B3 and lower B5 by the same amount.

A smaller goal needs fewer tags. With the B3 feeder meter tagged, every hidden shift of B3 must appear in the residual of that meter. The test then allows a shift of at most 3.07 kW, far below the 46.07 kW that the shedding needs. With the less accurate B3 building meter tagged instead, the bound is 13.33 kW.

The engineering side: Security over the life cycle

The devices of a cyber-physical system run for 15 to 30 years, far longer than office computers. A patch must be tested against the plant before it is installed, often in a planned outage. Security therefore cannot rely on updates alone. It starts with a list of every device and every connection, and it continues with daily practice [3].

PracticeWhy it matters on the campus
A complete list of devices, firmware versions and connectionsNobody can protect a Modbus gateway that nobody knows about
Separate accounts with the least rights that a task needsThe account of a technician should not change the setpoints of the battery
Logs of logins, changes and alarms, kept outside the control networkAfter an incident the logs show what happened, even if the attacker cleaned the controllers
Tested plans for an incident, with backups of every programThe campus can return to a known state within hours
Signed firmware and checked suppliersA forged update would bypass every other layer

Learning machines for attack detection

A hidden injection passes the chi-square test, but it may still look strange: The dormitory B3 rarely draws 154 kW on a mild spring evening. Machine learning can learn such patterns from many examples of normal and attacked readings. Ozay and colleagues compared several learning methods on test systems of the power grid. In their framework the methods detected attacks better than detectors that rely on state estimation [13].

A learned detector, however, can be attacked itself. Goodfellow and colleagues showed that small, carefully chosen changes of an input can flip the decision of a neural network [14]. An attacker who knows the detector can shape the injection so that it passes both the test and the network. Learned detectors therefore need their own defences, and the physics of the plant remains the anchor of the check.

A detector that is attacked itself

Suppose that the campus trains a network on a year of readings to flag unusual combinations. An attacker who obtains a copy of the network searches for a hidden injection that the network also accepts, for example by shifting load between buildings in small steps. The campus answers with tagged meters for the critical feeders and uses the network as one more layer, not as the only one.

Confidentiality, integrity, availability
The three goals of information security; a cyber-physical system weighs integrity and availability highest [3].
Attack surface
All entry points through which an attacker can reach a system: Buses, brokers, meters, feeds and maintenance access.
Deception attack
Sends false readings or false commands [1].
Denial of service (DoS)
Blocks or delays the true readings or commands [1].
Attack space
The resources of an attacker: Model knowledge, disclosure and disruption [4].
False data injection (FDI)
Changes readings so that the state estimate moves [8].
Hidden injection
\(\mathbf{a} = H\mathbf{c}\): The estimate moves by \(\mathbf{c}\) and the residual stays the same, so the chi-square test sees nothing.
Critical meter
A meter that no combination of the other meters can replace; its residual is always 0.
Replay attack
Records readings of normal operation and plays them back while the attacker acts [9].
Physical watermark
A small secret random input whose response a replay cannot show [6].
Response g
The slope of the movement of the measurement against the watermark, divided by \(\beta\); about 1 in normal operation and 0 under a replay.
HMAC
The hash-based message authentication code: A tag that only holders of the secret key can compute [11].
Counter against replay
A number in every message that must exceed the last accepted one.
Protected meters
Meters that the attacker cannot change; no hidden attack remains when their rows of \(H\) reach full rank [12].
Zones and conduits
Groups of devices of equal trust and the defined paths between them [10].
Adversarial example
A small, crafted change of an input that flips the decision of a learned model [14].
